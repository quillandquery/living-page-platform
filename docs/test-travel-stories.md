## The Reef at Havelock

We left Havelock before the sun, the sea still the colour of slate. Biscuit, the boat dog, sat at the bow like a figurehead and refused to look at anyone.

The divemaster promised us the reef at the edge of the drop-off, where the sand ends and the blue goes on forever.

For an hour it was perfect. Turtles. A horizon with nothing on it. The kind of quiet that makes you forget you have a body.

Then the engine died.

Not sputtered. Died. One moment a steady thrum under the deck, the next a silence so total it felt wrong.

We were a long way from shore. The tide had turned while we weren't paying attention, the way the important things always do.

Very reassuring. Very fine.

The divemaster's face did something I didn't like, and I made myself breathe, slow, the way they teach you before a descent.

Biscuit, meanwhile, had decided the whole thing was beneath comment and gone to sleep.

It took three hours for the other boat to find us. By then the sun was high and ridiculous and we had stopped pretending to be worried.

I remember the exact moment the engine caught again, the thrum returning like a heartbeat, Biscuit suddenly awake and furious at the noise.

We never saw the reef. Some trips you take to arrive. This one I took to learn how long three hours can be.

## The 28 to Nowhere

I got on the last tram in Lisbon without checking where it went, which is either the best or the worst way to see a city after dark.

It climbed into Alfama, the old quarter, where the streets are too narrow for anything but rumour and the streetlamps lean like they've had a long night of their own.

Somewhere a woman was singing fado through an open window, that particular grief that sounds like it is enjoying itself.

I got off at a corner I couldn't name.

The map on my screen gave up immediately. No signal, no sense, just a blue dot spinning in the middle of nothing.

Cobblestones everywhere, polished by a century of feet. A cat the colour of smoke watched me from a step and clearly knew the way.

Very brave. Very lost.

Then the panic came.

The ordinary traveller's panic, and I followed the music back up through Alfama because it was the only thing in that whole midnight maze with a direction.

The street simply ended at a terrace, and the whole city fell away beneath me, the river and the lights and the bridge throwing its reflection into the black water.

I stood there until my heart slowed down and the fear turned into the other thing, the good one, the one you travel for.

The last tram had long gone. I walked back down in the dark, and I have never been so happy to be nowhere.

## The Road to Muktinath

My brother Arjun had wanted to walk to Muktinath for twenty years, and in the end I walked it for both of us, six months after the funeral.

The trail climbs out of the last of the trees into a country of stone and wind, where the air thins until every step is a small negotiation with your own lungs.

At that altitude the sky does something I have no good words for. The stars don't twinkle. They burn, steady and close, the whole cold machinery of the universe hung low enough to touch.

I carried a photo of Arjun in my chest pocket.

Each night I took it out and let him look at where we'd got to. It felt less like grief and more like company.

Very rational. Very sane.

On the fourth day I stopped being able to breathe properly, and a grandmother running a teahouse made me sit, and fed me garlic soup, and told me without ceremony that the mountain decides, not me.

Arjun would have loved her.

The pass, when it finally came, nearly broke me.

Just a line of prayer flags shredding themselves in the wind, and a view that went on until the curve of the earth took it away.

I left the photo under a stone.

Not because I wanted to let him go, but because he had arrived, and some part of me understood he didn't need carrying any further.

I came down alone, and lighter, and the stars that night were almost unbearable.

## The Mirror at Uyuni

Nobody tells you that the salt flats of Uyuni stop being a place and become a rumour about one.

After the rains, a skin of water turns the whole plateau into a mirror, and the sky falls into the ground, and you lose any sense of which way is up. It is the most surreal thing I have ever stood inside.

We drove out at first light in a battered jeep, four strangers and a driver named Wilson who had done this ten thousand times and still went quiet when the horizon disappeared.

There is no horizon. That is the thing.

Cloud below you, cloud above, your own reflection walking upside down beneath your feet. It is the closest I have come to floating, weightless, without ever leaving the ground.

Wilson cut the engine in the middle of all that white and told us to get out and say nothing.

So we stood in the impossible, each of us alone in the exact centre of a sphere of sky. The whole thing had the logic of a dream you are having while awake.

Very grounded. Very normal.

I don't know how long we were out there. Time doesn't have much to hold onto when the world runs out of edges.

At some point I realised I had been holding my breath, as if breathing might crack the surface.

Then the spell broke.

One bird crossing, miles up or inches down, impossible to say, and we laughed like children, and Wilson started the engine.

I have seen a lot of places since. None of them have made me feel so gently, dreamily erased.

## Nino's Kitchen

I booked a room in a village in the Georgian mountains expecting a bed, and got adopted instead.

Nino was seventy, four foot something, and ran her guesthouse the way a captain runs a ship that has never once sunk.

The kitchen was the whole house, really. Everything happened at a long table under a window, with a wood stove breathing in the corner and bread that appeared faster than anyone could eat it.

Nino spoke no English. I spoke no Georgian.

We managed entirely through food and eyebrows, which turned out to be plenty.

On the first night she poured a clear spirit called chacha and watched, delighted, as it took the roof off my mouth.

Very civilised. Very measured.

By the third day I had been shown photographs of every grandchild, corrected on my chopping technique, and folded into the rhythm of the house as if I had always been there.

Then the power went out across the whole valley.

Nino didn't even look up. She lit two candles, put the kettle on the stove, and the kitchen simply carried on, warm and golden, as if the twentieth century were an optional extra.

We sat late by the fire while the rain found the roof.

I understood almost nothing anyone said, and I have rarely felt so completely at home.

When I left she pressed a jar of something into my hands and held my face for a second, and I had to breathe carefully on the bus down the mountain.
